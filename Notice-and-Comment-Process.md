# Notice and Comment Proccess

**Author:** Sohum Pohane

What stood out to me most was the "notice and comment" process. This is supposed to be the chance for the public to weigh in on agency rules, but is really just industry insiders, lobbyists, and trade associations communicating with each other. The whole point of APA's comment period is that regular people get a say before a rule becomes binding. But if it is just industry against industry, what is the point of having this "public" comment. A well known example involves EPA rules on toxic air pollutants, where industry groups were the vast majority of the comments submitted, while public interest groups were a vast minority of it. This is a big gap, and when you truly think about it, it makes sense in that writing a comment to an agency isn't as simple as leaving a review. It takes lawyers, technical experts, and more.

Agencies respond better to elaborate data backed comments rather than some simple letter from a normal person. Companies have become experts at this in that it makes the process slower and more complicated and if they have the resources to wait it out, the final decision will be closer to what they want. During this, the public interest groups don't have the money to be able to compete with these companies. One law review article I found made it clear to me and it said that if you build a process where showing up costs real time and money, you shouldn't be surprised when it's dominated by whoever has the most time and money. It's easy to forget this even though APA is shown as a neutral act.

Now the question is how much change do industry comments actually do. Some political scientists who have compared draft rules to the final published rules found that industry comments usually only produce small changes, not complete rewrites. There's also research that says the real influence happens earlier, before the rules are even drafted, a stage where businesses lobby agencies about what to actually regulate. Therefore it's hard to tell where the influence is exactly and how much influence they have. There's also a problem where advocacy groups will flood an agency with several near identical form letter comments. Agencies can't read all these duplicate comments, so from my understanding they end up prioritizing whoever frames their objections in the agency's own technical language. So it would be of use to have the technical or legal expertise to be able to make these comments in a way that the agency will actually see them.

The licensed professions like engineering or healthcare have licensing boards that go through this same notice and comment process when requirements are changed. If a state board proposes new requirements or rules, the existing professional association is going to show up with comments while the students and other people who it affects won't have an org ready to lobby for them. APA fixed the problem of secret rulemaking but it didn't fix the problem of unequal representation for these problems. Even though everyone can see the processes, they can't meaningfully participate in it if they aren't backed by orgs.
