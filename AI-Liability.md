# Who Pays for AI when it Fails?

**Author:** Sohum Pohane

AI has become a new source of failure as it's not just a part of the risk management team and a support for defending. It is a new weapon for attackers and it's a problem for courts now. A question worth asking is that when an AI system fails or turns against an organization, who pays. Liability should follow control in my opinion. The people that design, deploy, and hold the power to override an AI should hold the risk it creates. They should also treat NIST and CISA as the working standard rather than some optional text to comply with.

Anthropic reported in mid September of 2025 it detected an operation by a Chinese state sponsored group that was trying to collect information targeting around 30 entities with some intrusions that were validated. It used the Claude Code tool to automate cyberattacks on global targets. Law firm analysis of the report says that AI executed 80 to 90 percent of the operations, and humans came in for key decisions for example the scope of data exfiltration. The point that is interesting here is that Anthropic is describing misuse of its own product, also people in the security industry were not clear on AI's actual role. Anthropic's September 2026 report describes that most of the operations it reviewed as enabled by AI execution or orchestration, with multi agent frameworks doing some type of exploitation or exfiltration while humans set targets. Attackers also know our defenses and are often better at using them. Automation makes this issue worse, if an attack is more consistent, an organization that doesn't use defensive automation might struggle to say that its precautions are reasonable.

GAO found that deploying AI may make critical infrastructure systems, like those that give water, electricity, and food more vulnerable. It also found that none of the sector risk assessments submitted by federal lead agencies fully address the six activities that lay the foundation for assessing AI risk. In December 2025, CISA and international partners gave guidance on AI in operational technology built around 4 steps, understand AI, assess its use in operational technology, establish governance, and embed safety and security, including oversight, transparency, and incident response. The guidance says risks include process models drifting over time and safety process bypasses. NIST's Cyber AI Profile NISTIR 8596, organizes AI cyber risk into three overlapping areas, securing AI systems, conducting AI enabled cyber defense, and thwarting AI enabled attacks. It is voluntary and built on CSF 2.0, but it looks like a preliminary draft. However this guidance doesn't bind anyone and leaves the liability to be determined by contracts and courts.

Contracts do most of the determining right now, and the precedent I found is not an AI case. The 2024 CrowdStrike outage came from an automated update pushed to several machines. Delta sued, saying it had losses above 500 million and 7000 canceled flights. In 2025, a Georgia court let the gross negligence claim go through, crediting allegations that the update wasn't tested and pushed without staged deployment. The court acknowledged the contractual liability cap and said that Georgia's economic loss rule sharply restricts recovery outside the contract. CrowdStrike said the cap is in the single digit millions. I couldn't confirm how the case was resolved from the ruling, but the lesson was that when a customer depends on a vendor's automated security tooling, the contract is the remedy and is usually capped. An AI driven defense product that misfires would probably have the same decision in the US.

Contracts and doctrine should tie liability to control. A vendor that decides when and how a model updates should not be able to cap consequences of skipping basic safeguards, and a customer that has the power to switch a system off should answer for if that power was used. Also NIST and CISA guidance should be a benchmark for reasonable care, this includes having documented governance, continuous testing, and a manual fallback. A strong, well supported CIO or compliance lead should also be the core of accountability. Also incident reporting should be taken more seriously. Reporting is not required and should be required, CIRCIA would require that covered entities report incidents within 72 hours but CISA must lay out those rules.
