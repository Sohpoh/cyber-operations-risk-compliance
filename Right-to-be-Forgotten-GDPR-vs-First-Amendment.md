# A Right to be Forgotten: GDPR Article 17 and the First Amendment

**Author:** Sohum Pohane

It is said that everything posted online lives forever. The European Union rejects that as a matter of law. Under Google Spain v. AEPD and Article 17 of the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), Europeans can compel search engines and other data controllers to delete or delist personal information, even when the information is true. The United States doesn't have an equivalent. American law treats the publication of truthful, lawfully obtained information as speech protected by the First Amendment. A general American right to be forgotten would be unconstitutional, but that leaves certain gaps.

The European right began with a dated debt notice. In 1998, a Spanish newspaper published announcements of a forced property sale tied to Mario Costeja González's social security debts. Years later, the notice still appeared when anyone searched his name even though the debt was resolved. In May 2014, the Court of Justice of the EU held that a search engine is a "data controller" responsible for personal information it indexes. Individuals can ask it to remove links to information that is inadequate or no longer relevant, even when the original publication was lawful and stays online at its source. The court acknowledged that the right must be balanced against the public's interest in information but said privacy generally prevails in ordinary cases.

The GDPR went into effect in 2018, which made this a statutory right. Article 17 allows for erasure when data is no longer needed, consent is withdrawn, processing was unlawful, or the data was collected from a child for an online service. Controllers that made the data public must take reasonable steps to notify other controllers. The right isn't absolute and doesn't apply where processing is necessary for freedom of expression, public health, archiving and research, or legal claims.

In the American perspective, truth is a defense to defamation but not to invasion of privacy. American law can punish the revealing of true facts. In practice, though, the Supreme Court has favored freedom of speech so strongly that a right to be forgotten like the one in the EU wouldn't survive in America. In Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, a reporter broadcast the name of a deceased rape victim found in public indictments, which violated a Georgia statute. The Court said that a state may not sanction the accurate publication of information from open judicial records, so essentially privacy interests fade once information is in the public record. The Florida Star v. B.J.F. extended this idea. A newspaper printed a sexual assault victim's name from a police report a sheriff's department had left in its pressroom. The Court said that if a news organization gets truthful information legally, which in this case it did, the government usually can't punish it for publishing it. Sorrell v. IMS Health extended that protection to data. Vermont had barred pharmacies from selling prescriber-identifying records for drug marketing. The Court said that the creation and sharing of information are speech, and therefore ruled that the Vermont law was unconstitutional. These cases make Google Spain seem like it wouldn't be possible in an American court.

US firms operating in Europe have to honor erasure requests there while publishing the same information freely in the US. In terms of underlying harms, the US tries to address this by controlling the source of information rather than its publication. For example, 25 states have at least one automatic record clearing provision and around 12 have fully automated clean slate laws. California's Delete Act created the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform (DROP), which sends one deletion request to more than 500 registered data brokers, who had to start respecting the requests on August 1, 2026. An important thing to note is that these tools regulate government records and commercial data trading, not the press or search results.

The question of whether we would want an aging Nazi war criminal to delete his youthful SS membership is in a way a flaw in the right to be forgotten. Someone must decide whose past deserves to fade, and the European model gives that judgment to search engines and regulators. American law rejects giving anyone that power, and gives preference to truthful information remaining available and to society deciding what to make of it. Controlling information at its source, rather than suppressing it downstream, is the better fit for a legal system built on the First Amendment.
